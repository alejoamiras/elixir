Plan · mega-deep tier · yacana-bridge · drafted and approved 2026-09-12

Yacana survives the next Aztec upgrade

When Aztec ships V6, everything on V5 stays on V5: balances, the miner, the token. This plan gives every holder a way out and a way forward, through Ethereum, one holder at a time — and a way for Yacana's mining schedule to continue on V6 as if nothing happened. It ships an Ethereum token (YACA), a portal that moves coins between versions, a rehearsal rig that upgrades a local Aztec network on demand, the app screens you picked on the design canvas, and the runbook you will follow on the day.

Where it stands

Three independent drafts (this session, Codex, a fresh Fable agent) were consolidated into one plan, then checked for contradictions, then audited twice by both model families. Every finding was verified against the Aztec 5.2.0 sources and the repo before it was accepted; the plan changed materially each round. The biggest reversals: the portal now records an upgrade the moment anyone touches it instead of trusting a timestamp Aztec's contracts cannot vouch for; the issuance cap became a fixed policy the multisig cannot dial; sends into a new version can only be pushed by their owner or by a key you list; the rehearsal never touches the live site. The final fresh-context verdict and your decisions are recorded below; the delta went back to the reviewer once more for a clean verdict.

Why this tier

The rubric scored six of six dimensions high: novelty (a cross-rollup migration nobody has built on Aztec), blast radius (every holder's balance), irreversibility (an ERC-20 and a portal on Ethereum, a rollup that stops), migration cost (the whole point), external coupling (Aztec governance, the Registry, Ethereum), security sensitivity (a mint on Ethereum). Two or more high plus high novelty is the rule for mega-deep: research agents before drafting, three drafts, two audit rounds with a split second round, a final fresh pass. Human context

What an upgrade does

Aztec's Alpha rollups are replaced, not migrated. A governance vote adds a new rollup to a registry on Ethereum; from then on the new one is "canonical". The old one keeps running only as long as its operators keep it running, with no notice when it stops. Nothing crosses by itself.

Why Ethereum is the road

Both versions can send messages to Ethereum and receive messages from it. A portal on Ethereum can consume a "burned 12 YACA" message from V5 and send a "mint 12 YACA" message into V6, in one transaction. That is the whole bridge. The same portal also mints an ordinary ERC-20, YACA, for anyone who would rather hold on Ethereum.

Send ahead

Before or after the flip, a holder burns their balance on V5 with a secret only they know. The burn is held on Ethereum until V6's miner exists, then forwarded; on V6 the holder claims it privately at sign-in. If V6 never comes, or they change their mind, the same held coins become YACA in their Ethereum wallet. A held coin is never stranded.

What can be lost

A send is safe once V5 has settled the epoch that contains it on Ethereum. Each send shows a deadline for that; a missed deadline undoes the burn (the coins are back on V5, unsent). Balances still sitting on V5 when V5 stops are lost — the app says so in plain words, stops mining on V5 the moment V6 is live, and the old app keeps a one-button page at v5.yacana.network for as long as V5 runs.

The guard rails on Ethereum

A compromised old rollup could try to fake burns. The portal limits what each version may ever issue: three times what its mining schedule could have produced, frozen the moment the upgrade is observed, minus nothing you deposited. The multisig can pause a version for up to 30 days at a time (60 in total) and every version's exits close for good six months after its flip or once the version after next is live, whichever is later. The multisig cannot change the cap, cannot repoint an old version, and holds no timelock — by your choice.

The rig

A local Aztec network is booted, a second rollup is deployed, a governance proposal is voted and executed with a time-warped clock, the old node is stopped, a new node is pinned to the new version, Yacana is deployed on it continuing V5's epoch count, and every crossing is exercised: to Ethereum, back from Ethereum, ahead into V6, ahead across two upgrades, a send that never settles, a send when V6 never comes. The runbook's commands are the rig's commands, so the day you need them they have already run.

Phases

Arc 1 — contracts

P1 · The protocol

The message formats, the per-send secrets and redeem keys, the test vectors shared by Noir, Solidity and TypeScript, and the toolchain pins for the Foundry binaries.

proves itself by: the same vectors passing in all three languages, lint, the toolchain hash test

P2 · The miner learns to bridge

Send ahead, exit to Ethereum, claim from Ethereum, retire (mining ends when the portal says so), and a constructor that continues the previous version's schedule.

proves itself by: the Noir test suite, the storage layouts and committed artifacts unchanged except where intended, the gate-count check

P3 · YACA and the portal

The ERC-20 and the portal on Ethereum, tested against Aztec's real message boxes rather than stand-ins: every crossing, the cap, the pauses, the deadlines, the signatures, the accounting invariants.

proves itself by: the Foundry suite, the committed ABI unchanged, a deploy on a throwaway local chain

Arc 2 — the rig

P4 · The flip alone

The local network upgrades itself from V5 to V6 to V7 with no Yacana involved: proposal, vote, execution, the old node stopped, the new node pinned and settling. Its wall time is measured and becomes the CI budget.

proves itself by: the flip case green locally; its pull-request check is written now and proves itself on the first pull request

P5 · Every migration case

Eleven scenarios end to end through the operator script, including the migration in the runbook's exact order, a send that is never settled and gets undone, a send when V6 is never registered, and a skipped version.

proves itself by: the full rig suite green locally, runnable on demand from CI

Arc 3 — the app

P6 · The plumbing in the miner

The Ethereum RPC setting, the bridge journal and its single queue, the wagmi wallet connection with its picker of installed wallets, the wallet vault's fingerprint, the fee payer abstraction.

proves itself by: unit and component tests, typecheck, the replay lane, a vault test on an existing sealed record

P7 · The guided path

The screens from the canvas: the migration card, the send-ahead sheet, the arrival card with its one-tap Claim, To Ethereum, Deposit from Ethereum, the exceptions. Copy updated for no relayer, no waiting period, no automatic sends, and the held card says who may forward a send and why.

proves itself by: component specs and a browser run on the rig that signs in on V5, sends ahead, flips, signs in on V6 and lands with real proofs

P8 · The old app

A single-purpose build at v5.yacana.network that can restore keys, send ahead and nothing else, with its role checked against where it runs.

proves itself by: both builds passing the site checks, a mismatched role refused, one passkey working on both origins

Arc 4 — stats, docs, rehearsal

P9 · Stats, the announcement, the FAQ

The bridge page under stats, Etherscan links, the landing's announcement line and the standalone FAQ page with the six panels, linked from the miner and the stats page.

proves itself by: component specs, the screenshot gate, the stats and landing browser runs

P10 · Docs and CI

The design doc and the operator runbook for the day Aztec announces V6, written for a session that knows nothing, step by step, each step a command the rig already ran.

proves itself by: lint on docs, workflows and shell; every runbook step naming a tested entrypoint

P11 · The testnet rehearsal

YACA and the portal on Sepolia, the testnet miner redeployed with the bridge, one exit to Ethereum, one deposit back, one send held — all verified on explorers from the branch's preview site. Nothing goes live on the apex until you merge.
