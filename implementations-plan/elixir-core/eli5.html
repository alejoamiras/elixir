Summary

The prototype we are replacing made people grind Poseidon2 hashes on their CPU and rewarded whoever bought the most GPUs. This plan makes the proof itself the work. A miner repeatedly proves a small fixed circuit with Barretenberg; the hash of each finished proof is a lottery ticket; a ticket below the current target wins one reward. Winners submit a private Aztec transaction that re-verifies the proof inside a circuit, mints the reward into a private balance, and counts one claim toward the epoch. Losers never touch the chain. Epochs close after a fixed number of wins, so the chain sees a bounded number of claims per epoch no matter how much proving power joins, and the target adjusts by how long those wins took, exactly the way Bitcoin adjusts on block times.

This plan delivers the contracts, a platform-agnostic miner core, a browser miner with an embedded wallet and sponsored fees, tests at every layer, and a testnet deployment soak. Native mining through aztec-accelerator, external wallets and mainnet come later on the same core.

Why this tier

Rubric: novelty high (first proof-of-proving mechanism on Aztec), blast radius medium, irreversibility high (an immutable mainnet token), migration cost low, external coupling medium, security sensitivity high (value plus privacy). Three highs map to deep. You chose mid; the plan records that and compensates with measured facts instead of a third independent draft. How mining works

Bitcoin mining versus ours, side by side

Bitcoin miners take the block header, append a random number (the nonce), and run SHA256 on it. The result is a 256-bit number. If it is below the target, the block is valid. "Leading zeros" is just a picture of that comparison: a target of 2256−k means the hash must start with at least k zero bits. Every attempt costs nanoseconds, so miners try quintillions per block, and every 2016 blocks the network compares how long those blocks took to two weeks and rescales the target by that ratio, capped at four times either way. Each valid block mints a fixed subsidy.

Ours is the same shape with one substitution: the thing you hash is a proof, and making that proof is the expensive step.

What the circuit is for

Nobody searches for anything inside the circuit. It is a fixed, boring computation, a chain of a few thousand Poseidon2 hashes, chosen only because it is easy to write, deterministic, and big enough that proving it takes seconds. The miner's search is over nonces, and every nonce costs one complete proof. Making Poseidon2 itself faster does not help anyone: evaluating the chain natively takes microseconds either way; what takes seconds is producing the proof of it. So the only way to get more tickets per hour is to prove faster, which is the point.

How the target gets set and moved, with numbers

Two knobs, and they are different things. The circuit size fixes the cost of one ticket for the life of a deployment, like SHA256's cost per hash. The target is what moves. Suppose one laptop proves about 1,000 tickets an hour and we want 24 wins an hour from it at launch: the initial target is set so each ticket wins with probability 24 ÷ 1,000, that is target = 0.024 × 2128. Now ten laptops join. Twenty-four wins arrive in six minutes instead of sixty, the ratio is 0.1, the clamp turns it into ÷4, and the next epoch's target is a quarter of the old one. That epoch takes 24 minutes; the ratio 0.4 is applied unclamped; the epoch after that takes about an hour again. Two epochs to absorb a tenfold surge, four for a hundredfold. Emission never changed: every closed epoch mints 24 × 4 = 96 ELX. And if everyone leaves, no 24th claim arrives; after four hours anyone can close the epoch, and the target becomes four times easier each time that happens until miners return.

Can the "re-prove the same inputs" shortcut be closed?

Not at our layer. Barretenberg's non-hiding proof flavour reserves the first four rows of every circuit for the hiding flavour's randomness and leaves them unconstrained; the verifier ignores them by design. Our circuit code cannot touch those rows, and hashing only parts of the proof does not help because every part depends on them. Switching to the hiding flavour makes it worse: re-randomising a proof is then the intended feature. A fix would be a small upstream change to Barretenberg (constrain or zero those rows in the non-hiding flavour), which we could propose but do not control. The practical impact is a constant factor: whoever patches the prover gets tickets at roughly half to two-thirds the honest cost, still paying a full sumcheck and opening per ticket, and still scaling with circuit size. Phase 1 builds the attack and measures the real number.

Human context

Why not just fix the old design?

Any lottery whose ticket a miner can compute without proving turns back into hash grinding. The old design's ticket was a hash of public inputs and a nonce, so GPUs win and Barretenberg is never touched. Here the ticket is the hash of the finished proof: there is no shortcut to it that does not involve producing the proof.

Why a lottery rather than "pay every proof"?

Paying every proof is simpler and needs no in-circuit verifier, but then every proof is a transaction, so chain load grows with participation. With the lottery, the chain only ever sees winners, at most 24 per epoch. Codex initially preferred an L1-based variant as the fallback; both fallbacks are written up in the plan and only used if Phase 1 says no.

What "private" means here

Nobody can tell who mined or how much anyone holds. What is visible: that a claim happened, its epoch, its (random-looking) winning ticket, the fixed reward amount, and when. A claim is recognisable as a mining claim.

What we are trusting

One known shortcut exists and is priced in: Barretenberg's non-hiding proofs leave four rows of the circuit unconstrained, so a modified prover can re-prove the same inputs with fresh values there and get a new ticket for roughly half to two-thirds the cost of an honest proof, reusing the heavy curve math. Phase 1 builds that attack and measures it. That number is what the attack costs, not a proof that nothing cheaper exists; the "nothing cheaper" part is a stated assumption handed to the external audit. A GPU prover for Barretenberg, if someone builds one, is a welcome outcome rather than an attack.

How the plan changed under review

Codex rejected the first version for two real problems: hour-based epochs let a sudden 100× surge mint roughly 3,000 rewards before difficulty caught up, and the one-epoch grace period rewarded miners who hid their wins. Version two counts claims instead of hours, which caps issuance by construction, and drops grace. Codex then conditionally approved and asked for exact arithmetic, a missing circuit input, harder spike thresholds and honest wording; all adopted. The independent Claude reviewer found the same-inputs shortcut above, showed the wallet cannot sign the message the secret derivation needed (so the secret is now a fresh random value per epoch), proved the emergency-close formula always collapsed to "four times easier" (now stated plainly, with a less predictable seed), and asked for the difficulty arithmetic to be tested at realistic launch values, for the verifier to live inline in the claim, and for the mining fee to be measured. All six conditions adopted.
